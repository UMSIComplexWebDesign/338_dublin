Reading the City

Taking a guided tour through Dublin is always an incredible experience, but walking the city with a cultural geographer turned the afternoon into something much more meaningful than sightseeing. Our class toured the Dublin Docklands with Dr. Darren Kelly, and instead of simply pointing out buildings, he helped us “read” the city as evidence of Ireland’s economic transformation.

Dr. Kelly repeated an idea that stayed with me throughout the tour: “To understand Ireland, you need to understand its past.” That made our starting point at the Custom House feel intentional. Completed in 1791, it once represented imperial trade and British administration. On our tour, it became a starting point for thinking about how power, economics, geography, and history have shaped Ireland.

Our Guide

Darren Kelly, PhD holds a BA in English and Geography and a PhD in Cultural Geography from Dublin City University. He lectures at Marino Institute of Education, Trinity College Dublin.

A Fulbright Scholar in Residence at Beloit College, Dr. Kelly is widely published and recognized for his expertise in field-based learning, study abroad, intercultural intelligence, and the relationship between academic theory and experiential learning.

From Emigration to Education

One of the most interesting parts of the tour was learning that Ireland’s economic rise did not begin with tech companies or glass office buildings. It began with education. In the 1980s, Ireland had many well-educated young people but not enough university places or jobs for them. Many had to leave Ireland to find work in Britain, the United States, or elsewhere.

Education is often presented as a direct path to opportunity. In Ireland’s case, the talent was there before the economy was ready to use it.

Over time, the Irish government invested more heavily in higher education, colleges, and technical training. This helped create a skilled workforce in areas such as engineering, pharmaceuticals, banking, and computing. At the same time, Ireland used low corporate tax rates and other incentives to attract multinational companies.

The IFSC and the Celtic Tiger

When we reached the IFSC, the International Financial Services Centre, this shift became easier to see. Created in the late 1980s, the IFSC represented Ireland’s move away from traditional manufacturing and toward finance, electronic services, and global business.

This transformation helped fuel the “Celtic Tiger,” Ireland’s period of rapid economic growth from the 1990s into the mid-2000s. Dr. Kelly showed us some of the first major apartment buildings connected to this new urban lifestyle, built for higher-paid workers who wanted to live near the expanding financial and tech districts.

The Docklands: A New Skyline

The most memorable moment was near the quay and the Samuel Beckett Bridge, where we compared older images of the Docklands with the skyline in front of us. In the photos, Grand Canal Dock looked empty, industrial, and unfinished. In front of us were glass offices, expensive apartments, and the headquarters of major global companies. It felt like we were standing between two versions of Dublin at once.

The Cost of Growth

The tour also made clear that economic success has not benefited everyone equally. The housing boom collapsed after the 2008 financial crisis. Ireland took on enormous banking debts, construction stalled, and unemployment rose. Some public and distressed property assets were later sold to private investors and global firms — one reason Dublin’s housing crisis is not just about supply and demand, but about who owns the city.
