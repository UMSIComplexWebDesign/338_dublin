Overview

Our session on front-end web development in library digital services was led by Anna Leary, Library Systems and Digital Services Coordinator at the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland. This was a shorter, more technical session, but it connected directly to a major theme of our course: access. Accessibility is not only a web design requirement — it shapes how people seek, discover, and use knowledge.

One of the main ideas that stood out was that libraries are built on equal access to information. Because of that, every design choice either supports or weakens that mission. A confusing button label, a missing heading, or an unclear error message might seem small, but it can determine whether someone successfully finds what they need or gives up entirely.

Our Speaker

Anna Leary is the Library Systems and Digital Services Coordinator at the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland. Her work focuses on digital collections, library systems, digitisation, curation, and making collections easier to access and use.

What our group appreciated about her talk was that she presented web development as part of library service, not just as a technical job. The front end of a website is often the place where users first interact with a library, so it has to work for people with different needs, devices, backgrounds, and levels of confidence.

What Are Library Digital Services?

At first, our class guessed that library digital services might include catalog searches, digitized archives, chatbots, access to paywalled materials, printers, citation tools, and databases. Anna expanded that definition by describing library digital services as a complex ecosystem of connected platforms — discovery systems and catalogues, archives and repositories, databases and resources, booking systems, and library websites.

Accessibility Challenges

A major challenge is that libraries do not always control the systems they use. Many platforms rely on outside vendors rather than in-house development. This can make accessibility difficult because libraries may have limited control over the core design, limited front-end programming knowledge, and older interfaces that still need to serve many different users. Vendor updates can also introduce new accessibility problems, called regressions.

The session made it clear that small choices can have a big impact. Ambiguous buttons, poor page structure, missing headings, and inaccessible error messages can all create barriers. These barriers do not always announce themselves — they show up as extra effort, confusion, loss of orientation, and a decline in user confidence.

Information Seeking and Accessibility

Anna connected accessibility to Information Seeking Behaviour theory. The point is not only to meet technical standards, but to design systems that match the real human experience of searching for information.

She mentioned several models, including Wilson’s Information Seeking Behaviour model, Kuhlthau’s Information Search Process, and Bates’ Berry Picking Model. These theories show that searching is rarely a straight path. Users explore, change direction, reformulate searches, and gradually build understanding. Accessible design supports that process instead of making it harder.

POUR

We also discussed accessibility through POUR: perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Users need to be able to notice the information, navigate the interface, understand what is happening, and rely on the system to work with different technologies.

Front-end accessibility in a library context is not just about making a website easier to use — it is about whether people can access knowledge at all.
