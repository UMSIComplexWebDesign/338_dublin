Accessibility Checklist

  • Exhibits and Objects: Small objects are mounted at or below 40 inches, ensuring they are at a comfortable seated eye level. Display cases are shallow enough to allow for clear viewing from above. While objects are visible, some description cards are placed flat — tilting these cards would improve readability for all visitors.
  • Labels and Text: Overall, the contrast and fonts of labels were well done and easy to read. There were issues with sizes at some points; broad descriptions were easy to decipher, but smaller image labels became extremely difficult. The Egypt room text was nearly impossible to read due to dim lighting. No audio or braille options were available.
  • Audio Visuals and Interactive: Features were inconsistent. Some exhibits offered audio guides while others lacked accessibility indicators. Video displays did not include captions, and interactive headphones appeared intended more for general use than accessibility support.
  • Pathway and Navigation: Accessible floor maps are widely available via pamphlets and wall-mounted stands. The route is not entirely step-free — several specialized areas (specifically the Egyptian room) contain awkward stairs or partial floor levels that lack ramps.
  • Seating and Furniture: Each museum room included at least one chair along with several low benches. Seating was accessible and placed along walls without obstructing pathways or exhibit views, but chairs and benches were relatively small and lacked armrests.
  • Safety and Content: Clearly marked exits, and fire alarms appeared to have both light and sound.

Accessibility Details & Stand-out Exhibits

Ramp to the sunken exhibit

A ramp leads down the three steps to the sunken area in the middle of the ground floor (exhibit 2).

Plentiful seating

Lots of seating areas on each floor — single cushioned chairs, wooden benches against walls, and chairs near interactive activities.

No lift to the second floor

There is no lift or elevator to get to the second floor. Some rooms were quite small or had stairs in them, so a wheelchair user may not be able to see every object up close.

Egyptian room inaccessibility

The Egyptian room is completely inaccessible to wheelchair users. There is no braille in many places, no seats, and the rooms are very dark.

Decorative Egyptian artifacts

Carvings on stone tablets and textiles displayed in cases.

Pottery on display

Old clay jars, pitchers, and pots painted in earth-toned colors.
